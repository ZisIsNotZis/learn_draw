# Image-only review

## Stream study

The action reads as watering a plant at both sizes: the can, continuous water line, soil and plant form a legible chain. The stream does reach the pot interior. There is no obvious broken contact or missed pot.

The weakest action detail is the grip. The hand is a stack of pale lobes laid across the top of the handle, with very little visible wrapping or finger separation. The handle's left segment appears to end on the can below the hand, while its right segment forms a much larger exposed arch. This can be understood as a stylized grip, but does not strongly convey a hand supporting the can's weight. It is a local ambiguity rather than an absent attachment.

The water lands exactly at the plant stem's lower end. The blue water line, green stem and soil converge into one junction, making the endpoint look attached to the stem rather than visibly falling onto a patch of soil. At thumbnail size the water can resemble a thin tube connecting the can and plant. A distinct landing point in open soil would make the action more natural. The stream's shallow arc and slight initial rise are stylized, but are not by themselves a decisive physical failure.

The raised arm has a readable shoulder-to-hand direction, but its upper contour flows into the wrist without a clear elbow or cuff. The pose is somewhat diagrammatic and stiff. The face looks out toward the viewer rather than toward the watering, so attention does not reinforce the action. These are expressive limitations, not proof that the pose is anatomically impossible.

The composition is readable and the warm clothing/can versus cool hair/window/pot palette is coherent. The huge empty upper window occupies much more area than the action. The long downward arm is cut off at the bottom, and the torso ends in an isolated rounded block; together these make the figure feel cropped from a larger scene rather than intentionally framed. The water's departure is almost on the central window mullion, which adds an avoidable junction behind the focal action.

Line treatment is uneven: the face and hair have dark detailed outlines, while the arm, can, plant and window are almost entirely flat silhouettes. This concentrates visual finish in the face despite the hand/can/plant being the narrative subject. The flat treatment itself is valid; it does not require realistic shading. The issue is the relative clarity and finish of the important contact compared with the facial detail.

## Handle study

The watering action remains legible. The can is more steeply tipped and the compact exposed handle sits closer to the hand, which makes the load feel easier to hold than in the stream study. There is no clear floating-hand gap. The hand still reads as pale overlapping pads across the handle rather than a fully convincing wrap, but the contact is credible for a flat illustration.

The can/handle grouping has one residual ambiguity: the exposed arch curves directly down into the right side of the can, while the other side is hidden under the hand and body silhouette. The loop is inferable, especially at full resolution, but at small size it is easy to see a hand sitting on a curved hook. This is a weaker depiction of grasp than a clearly visible thumb/finger overlap, not a serious structural impossibility.

The water still ends at the same plant-stem junction, so the tube-like endpoint remains the clearest action defect. Its origin is now just to the left of the central mullion, which gives the can-to-water transition somewhat more separation than in the stream study, although the nearby vertical bar still competes with this small contact.

Pose, gaze, framing, palette and the mismatch between outlined face/hair and unoutlined action props have the same strengths and limitations as the stream study. The pose does not become substantially more expressive merely through the altered can angle. The illustration works as a simple instructional scene; the missing volume, sparse leaves, simplified sleeve and lack of cast shadows are acceptable stylistic simplifications. They should not be confused with the more consequential questions of a readable grip, a distinct stream landing, and purposeful framing.

Neither image has a catastrophic action or grouping failure. The handle study is the more convincing hand/can grouping. Both would benefit most from clarifying the water/soil contact and giving the action area the same deliberate visual attention as the face.
