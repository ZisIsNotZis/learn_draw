# Stream study

The action reads as watering a potted plant at both sizes. The stream visibly joins the spout and ends inside the soil ellipse. Its initial near-horizontal trajectory, slight rise and subsequent fall are plausible for a flowing jet; the rise alone is not a defect. The continuous thin line is an ordinary flat-illustration convention. There is no obvious spill outside the pot or disconnected liquid.

The main weakness is the grip. The hand lies over the upper end of the handle, but the pale finger shapes and ochre handle overlap do not clearly demonstrate a secure wrap. The can looks suspended from the hand, yet the exact contact is ambiguous at full size and becomes a small pale lump at thumbnail size. This is a local contact-clarity issue rather than a failure of the whole watering action.

The raised arm connects recognizably to the shoulder and hand, but its broad sleeve narrows into the hand with very little wrist articulation. The nearly frontal face looks toward the viewer rather than the plant or pouring point. That weakens the sense of attending to the task without making the action impossible.

The can and water group remain readable against the window. The window's central upright passes close behind the spout/stream join, adding some visual congestion there. The plant stem and the stream terminate at essentially the same spot on the soil, so water appears directed at the stem base; this is understandable, though separating those silhouettes slightly would improve clarity.

The graphic finish is coherent enough for a simple illustration, with clean large silhouettes and restrained colors. The face and hair carry substantially more contour detail than the sleeves, hand and watering can. That mismatch makes the weak hand construction stand out. The heavy flat sill and the torso's lower crop feel abrupt, but neither is a serious spatial defect. The lack of shadows or fabric folds is a valid stylistic choice.

# Handle study

The watering action also reads clearly here: a tilted can, connected stream, and landing point within the pot. The stream is slightly flatter than in the other study, but still describes a plausible jet. Its connection to the spout is visible at full size and retains the intended gesture at thumbnail size.

The compact handle brings the can closer to the hand and gives a more immediate impression of holding it than the long arch in the stream study. However, the precise grip remains unresolved: the fingers form a horizontal pale band above the handle, and the handle seems to emerge from beneath that band without an unmistakable encircling finger. The thumb/finger separation is too slight to explain the contact fully. This is the most consequential remaining drawing weakness, though it does not destroy readability.

The can body, handle and upper wrist are tightly grouped. Some edges are lost where the sleeve meets the can, and the handle's lower attachment disappears into the same yellow silhouette. Those losses can be acceptable flat-art simplification, but together they make the object feel somewhat like a yellow cutout attached to the sleeve instead of a distinctly gripped vessel. There is no need for realistic shading; a clearer ordering of the existing contours would carry the relationship.

The pose, frontal gaze, plant/stream convergence, and finish observations from the stream study apply here too. The arm is a broad continuous sweep with little elbow or wrist explanation. This is a simplified pose, not an anatomical impossibility. The unshaded pot, flat soil ellipse and stylized leaf arrangement are consistent illustration conventions and are not defects in themselves.

# Severity

Neither image has a clear catastrophic failure of pouring, contact with the soil, or scene grouping. Both communicate the intended activity at the small viewing size. The hand/handle contact deserves attention before further finish work; the frontal gaze and uneven detail distribution are secondary expressive and stylistic weaknesses. Adding general detail or lighting would not by itself resolve the grip.
